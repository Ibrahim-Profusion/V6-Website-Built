Your research needs to serve that voice. **Marketing will hate anything that reads like a generic "energy is expensive!" filler piece.** They want the actual mechanics: which policy, which market event, which infrastructure decision, drove each price move.

- Highlight events like: nuclear plant closures, coal→gas transition, storm-hardening surcharges, renewable portfolio standards, grid modernization dockets

### 2. Natural Gas (residential heating)
- Retail rate per therm or per Mcf — 2005 → 2025
- Wholesale price context (Henry Hub spot + regional basis differentials — New England has been famously constrained)
- Distinguish "commodity" cost from "delivery" cost
- Note events like: shale boom (post-2008), 2021 winter squeeze, 2022 Ukraine war spike, pipeline expansion fights (especially in New England)

### 3. Heating Oil (mostly Northeast)
- Retail heating oil price per gallon — 2005 → 2025
- Tied to global crude prices — note crude oil correlations
- ~5.3 million U.S. households use heating oil, ~85% of them in the Northeast
- Note: not regulated by state PUCs — retail dealer market
- Data source: EIA Heating Oil and Propane Update (weekly Oct-Mar)

### 4. Gasoline (regular retail)
- Regular grade retail gasoline price per gallon by state — 2005 → 2025
- Note East Coast vs national average — East Coast has structural refining constraints
- Note effects of RGGI (Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative) on regional pricing
- Data source: EIA weekly retail gasoline prices

## The time horizon: 2005 → 2025 (20 years)

We want the full arc. **Key era markers to structure the narrative around:**

- **2005–2008**: Peak oil scare era. Natural gas prices at all-time highs. Nuclear renaissance discussion.
- **2008–2009**: Great Recession + shale gas boom starts (Marcellus especially)
- **2011**: Fukushima → nuclear retreat begins. Vermont Yankee scheduled for closure.
- **2012–2015**: Shale gas depresses electricity generation costs. But New England pipeline capacity fails to keep up.
- **2014–2015**: Northeast "polar vortex" winters expose LNG dependence.
- **2016–2019**: RGGI expands. Multiple Northeast nuclear plants close (Vermont Yankee 2014, Pilgrim 2019, Indian Point 2020–21).
- **2020**: COVID-19 crashes demand + prices briefly.
- **2021**: Post-COVID demand rebound + Winter Storm Uri (Feb 2021) exposes natural gas vulnerabilities.
- **2022**: Russia invades Ukraine → global energy price shock. New England gas + electric prices spike ~2-3x.
- **2023–2024**: RGGI clearing prices at record highs. Multiple utilities file large rate cases citing storm-hardening + IRA-driven transmission needs.
- **2025**: Where we are now. AI data center demand starting to push wholesale power prices in some regions.

Your job is to map each of these events to concrete price impacts in each state, per energy type, with citations.

## Underlying causes to investigate (the "why" behind every price move)

The marketing team needs the *causal story*, not just the numbers. Please investigate and explain each of these, per state where relevant:

### Structural / market-design causes
- **ISO-NE grid constraints** (New England imports significant electricity, especially in winter, and pays capacity market premiums for winter-reliable generation)
- **Hydro-Québec imports** — a huge chunk of Northeast electricity in cold weather. Explain the political + infrastructure fights over transmission (Northern Pass killed in NH, NECEC / CMP corridor in ME fought at the ballot box, Champlain Hudson Power Express in NY approved and building)
- **PJM interconnection queue** — affects NJ, MD, MI (Consumers is MISO but neighbors PJM). AI data center demand starting to drive PJM prices up dramatically in 2024–25.
- **NYISO capacity zone pricing** — Downstate NY (esp. NYC + Hudson Valley) pays a huge premium due to transmission bottleneck
- **Nuclear plant closures** and their replacement generation (usually natural gas), especially:
  - Vermont Yankee (VT, closed 2014)
  - Pilgrim (MA, closed 2019)
  - Indian Point (NY, closed 2020–21)
  - Millstone (CT) — still operating but subsidized
- **Coal plant retirements** — especially DTE Monroe (MI), Brayton Point (MA, 2017), etc.
- **RGGI (Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative)** — cap-and-invest program covering CT, ME, MA, NH, NJ, NY, RI, VT, MD, VA, DE, PA. Explain what it adds to wholesale power prices and how it's changed.
- **Natural gas pipeline capacity in New England** — the "why" behind winter gas price spikes. Kinder Morgan NED cancelled, Constitution cancelled, Access Northeast cancelled. New England is more constrained today than 10 years ago.

### Infrastructure / physical causes
- **Aging distribution infrastructure** — many Northeast poles + wires are 60+ years old. Storm hardening + undergrounding is expensive.
- **Extreme weather events driving rate cases** — post-Sandy (NJ/NY), post-Ida, ice storms in New England
- **Electrification** load growth — heat pumps, EVs adding to system peak
- **Wildfire risk mitigation** — not a big factor in our states yet but worth mentioning

### Policy / regulatory causes
- **State clean energy mandates** (RPS / CES). Note NY's CLCPA, MA's 2050 mandate, CT's zero-carbon-by-2040, MD's ambitious 2035 target
- **Net metering / solar tariff shifts** (multiple states have changed how they credit rooftop solar over 2015–2025)
- **Public benefit charges / systems benefit charges** on bills
- **Storm hardening / grid modernization dockets** — often the largest driver of recent distribution charge increases
- **Utility ROE (return on equity) decisions** — how PUCs set what utilities can earn
- **Decoupling** — utilities in some states now earn revenue independent of volume sold, which changes incentive structures

### Fuel-market causes
- **Henry Hub + regional basis differentials** — the natural gas commodity story
- **Global crude oil price** — drives heating oil and gasoline in lockstep with OPEC decisions, geopolitics
- **LNG exports** — U.S. is now the world's largest LNG exporter (post-2016), which links our domestic gas price more tightly to global demand
- **Refinery closures on the East Coast** — Philadelphia Energy Solutions closed 2019, PBF idling capacity, structural short of gasoline supply on East Coast

### The specific "Canadian imports get taxed more" theme Ibrahim mentioned
Ibrahim believes New England electricity is more expensive partly because a chunk of it comes from Canada (Hydro-Québec) and gets taxed at the border. Please investigate the accuracy of this claim rigorously. What's likely closer to the truth:
- Canadian hydro is *cheaper* wholesale, but transmission capacity into New England is constrained
- Tariffs on Canadian electricity are minimal or zero (energy trade is largely free between US and Canada under the USMCA/CUSMA framework and pre-existing tariff schedules)
- The premium New England pays is more about **capacity constraints, winter reliability payments, and the fact that when Hydro-Québec is fully utilized, the marginal generator setting the wholesale price is often natural gas**
- Also note: Trump administration has floated tariffs on Canadian goods including energy in 2024–25 policy discussions; where any of this became real vs. just proposed matters and needs current-as-of-your-research-date facts

Give a factual, sourced answer even if it partly contradicts the premise.

## Research protocol (please follow this in order)

### Step 1: Verify the state list + utility list
Cross-reference the 11 states + utilities table above against current facts. If any utility name has changed (e.g., National Grid Rhode Island became Rhode Island Energy in 2022), note it and use the current name in your doc.

### Step 2: Pull the primary data
EIA is your main friend here. Specifically:
- **Electricity:** EIA Form 861 (annual retail sales + revenue by state + utility) and the "Electric Power Monthly" tables → residential price data 2005–present
- **Natural gas:** EIA Natural Gas monthly data → residential price by state
- **Heating oil:** EIA "Heating Oil and Propane Update" (weekly Oct–Mar historical archives)
- **Gasoline:** EIA weekly retail gasoline prices, by PADD and by state

For each state × energy-type combination, get the 2005 price and the most-recent-available price. Compute the percent change. Report both the raw number and the CPI-adjusted "real" change.

### Step 3: Investigate the underlying causes
For each significant price movement (e.g., a 40%+ increase over 5 years), figure out *why*. This is where the state PUC/PSC websites, news archives, and industry publications matter. Don't just say "prices went up" — say "prices went up because [Utility X] won a $Y rate case on [date] citing storm-hardening spending, plus a wholesale price spike driven by pipeline capacity constraints in winter 2022."

### Step 4: Interview the marketing team's need
As you write, keep asking yourself: "Would ProFusion's marketing manager be able to write a killer 300-word blog post from this section alone?" If the answer is no — add more specifics, more numbers, more names, more dates.

### Step 5: Cite everything
Inline citations `[EIA, 2024]` or footnote-style. A source index at the bottom. If you can't find a source for a claim, either dig harder or drop the claim.
